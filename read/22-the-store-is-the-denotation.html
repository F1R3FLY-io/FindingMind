Chapter 22

The Store Is the Denotation

22.1 Two senses of “runs”

Everything in the Machinery part is a construction on a language presentation. The reader has been told, repeatedly, that the constructions are not metaphors: that a cost-accounted theory meters, that a graded clause resolves a race, that a \(\dok\) intervenes. Each of those verbs has two senses. In the first, “the theory meters” means that the rewrite relation, read as a mathematical object, has a component that decreases. In the second, it means that a machine somewhere is doing the subtracting, and that if one pulled the plug the subtracting would stop. The Pledge promised the second sense when it said that the network is the computer and pointed at the Groq and Cerebras dies and the FPGAs whose fabric comes and goes. This part is where the second sense is delivered, and it is delivered in two installments of very different maturity.

The first installment is RSpace. It is a data structure, an algorithm over it, and a production store with several years of operation behind it, and this chapter’s claim is that the store is the denotation: that the compilation of rho into RSpace states is, term for term, a denotational semantics, with the structural laws of the calculus holding as literal equalities of hashtables. The second installment, in the next chapter, is a compiler from rho through a name-free combinator calculus to a matrix machine that runs on a graphics processor. It exists, it passes its tests, and it has not yet been measured on anything that matters; we will say exactly how nascent it is. The two are realizations of the same COMM rule on two hardware philosophies that reward opposite things, and the third chapter of the part asks what that opposition prices.

A word about the voice. The Machinery part spoke of theories; this part speaks of a particular implementation, f1r3node, written in Rust, and of a particular compiler, Saph1r3. Where the book says what the code does, it has been checked against the code and its documentation at the time of writing; where it says what the code’s authors intend, it says so. The book does not become a manual here. It becomes a report on whether the abstractions of the preceding part are abstractions of anything, and the answer is that one of them has been running in production for some time and the other was compiled for the first time this autumn.

22.2 The store

Modulo structural congruence the parallel fragment of rho, \((\Par, \nil)\), is the free commutative monoid, and the free commutative monoid on a set is the finite-multiset construction on it. Interpreting \(\Par\) as multiset union therefore makes the denotation a monoid homomorphism on the nose, and structural congruence of the parallel skeleton becomes literal equality of multisets — the tightest fit syntax and semantics can have. Bucketing the multiset by channel turns it into a hashtable. That hashtable is RSpace.

Definition 22.1 Tables¶

A table is a finite partial map from names to polarity-homogeneous multisets of payloads: a key holds either a bag of output payloads \(x \mapsto \{\!|\,Q_1,\dots,Q_m\,|\!\}^{+}\) — produces, data — or a bag of input continuations \(x \mapsto \{\!|\,(y_1)P_1,\dots,(y_n)P_n\,|\!\}^{-}\) — consumes, abstractions — never a mixture. The keys are names, hence quoted processes: RSpace is content-addressed, and a channel’s identity is the hash of the process it quotes.

Definition 22.2 The RSpace denotation¶

\[\begin{align*} \sem{\nil}&=\varnothing, & \sem{x!(Q)}&=\{\,\sem{x}\mapsto\{\!|\sem Q|\!\}^{+}\,\},\\ \sem{P\Par Q}&=\sem P \uplus_{k} \sem Q, & \sem{\fork(y\leftarrow x)P}&=\{\,\sem{x}\mapsto\{\!|(y)\sem P|\!\}^{-}\,\},\\ \sem{*x} &= *\sem{x}, & \sem{@P} &= @\sem{P}, \end{align*}\] where \(\uplus_k\) is key-wise multiset union: the bags \(P\) and \(Q\) install at a key are united. Each prefix installs a singleton bag; \(\Par\) co-locates produces and consumes. A key whose two operands carry the same polarity stays homogeneous; a key receiving both polarities is a redex.

Proposition 22.1 Exact homomorphism¶

\(\sem{-}\) is a homomorphism of commutative monoids: \(\sem\nil=\varnothing\) and \(\sem{P\Par Q}=\sem P\uplus_k\sem Q\). Hence the parallel laws of structural congruence — associativity, commutativity, unit — hold as literal equalities of tables, and \(P\equiv Q \Rightarrow \sem P=\sem Q\).

Proof

\(\uplus_k\) inherits the commutative-monoid laws of multiset union key-wise, and the empty table is the unit. The prefix clauses are singletons, so reassociating or commuting a parallel composition reassociates or commutes the corresponding bag unions, which multiset equality absorbs. Multiset union has no idempotence to defeat: \(a!(0) \Par a!(0)\) installs a two-element bag at \(a\), distinct from the one-element bag of \(a!(0)\). Counts are kept.

The reflection law \(*@P \equiv P\) is validated separately, and the source for this section [75] derives it rather than assuming it: in a universe stratified into two sorts of set and two sorts of atom, with an involution swapping the colors, quotation is a seal followed by a swap, dereference undoes it, and \(*@P \equiv P\) is the statement that the swap is its own inverse. The book does not need the stratification and does not rehearse it. What it needs is that every generator of structural congruence holds in the table model — the parallel laws on the nose and reflection by the involution — so that a table is a well-defined function of a process modulo \(\equiv\).

Definition 22.3 Eager reaction: the zip¶

A key \(x\) carrying both a produce-bag \(\{\!|Q_1,\dots,Q_m|\!\}^{+}\) and a consume-bag \(\{\!|(y_1)P_1,\dots,(y_n)P_n|\!\}^{-}\) is a redex. Eager reaction fires it: choose underlying lists of the two bags and zip them; each matched pair reacts by application, \((y_i)P_i \cdot Q_i = P_i\{@Q_i/y_i\}\), and the resulting process is merged by \(\uplus_k\) back into the table; the \(|m-n|\) unmatched payloads of the longer bag remain at \(x\) as a homogeneous surplus. Its normal forms are the tables with no mixed-polarity key.

Which \(Q_i\) meets which \((y_j)P_j\), and which payloads survive as surplus, depend on the chosen list orderings. This is rho’s choice of which send meets which receive, now localized at the key — the resolver of Chapter 13, made into a datum one can point at. The denotation \(\sem{-}\) remains a function (plain \(\uplus_k\), no firing); reaction is the separate dynamics, a relation on tables confluent only up to the equivalence that forgets the pairing. And the minimal context \([\,]\Par x!(Q)\) that Chapter 17 uses to label a transition is exactly the minimal store insertion — one produce at key \(x\) — that turns a pending consume into a redex. Context labels are RSpace puts.

Remark 22.1 Store monad plus zip¶

The table functor carries a monad structure — the finite-multiset monad, lifted key-wise — and RSpace is this monad together with eager reaction, presented as a distributive law of the reaction over the store: a bialgebra in the sense of Turi and Plotkin [86]. This is the precise content of the description one hears of RSpace as “strictly more than a monad”: the monad is the store, the surplus is the zip. Whether the bialgebra laws hold — whether eager reaction is confluent up to resource bisimilarity, whether the distributive law is one — is filed in the source as an obligation and not a theorem [75, §8]. The book reports it so.

22.3 Two layers, and what each is adequate for

Forget multiplicities and flatten a table into its set of context-labeled transitions — a consume at \(x\) is an offer-input, a produce an offer-output, a mixed key an internal redex — and one has a natural transformation \(\pi\) from tables to the behavior functor of Chapter 17. The RSpace model sits above the behavioral model and projects onto it: \[\text{Syntax} \xrightarrow{\ \sem{-}\ } \text{Tables (RSpace)} \xrightarrow{\ \pi\ } \text{Behaviors},\] and the composite is the context-behavior denotation the rest of the book works in.

Definition 22.4 Resource bisimilarity¶

Two tables are resource bisimilar when they are bisimilar as coalgebras of the table functor: same keys, same multiplicities at each key, and matching successors under eager reaction. It refines context bisimilarity, with \(\pi\) the quotient that forgets multiplicity and keys.

The source states two full-abstraction theorems [75]: at the resource layer, resource bisimilarity coincides with equality in the final coalgebra of the table functor; at the behavioral layer, context bisimilarity coincides with equality in the final coalgebra of the behavior functor, and because the labels are contexts the behavioral equivalence is a congruence by construction, so full abstraction holds for a congruence with no closure step. Both proofs are sketches, and the obligations section of the source lists what would discharge them: existence of the final coalgebras at the right size, eager confluence, the bialgebra laws, and weak pullback preservation for the functors involved. We state the theorems as the source states them and at the confidence the source has.

Why two layers, and not one? Because they answer different questions. The resource layer is the machine: it knows that there are two messages at \(a\) and not one, it knows which key is which, and it is what a validator must reproduce to the byte when it replays a block. The behavioral layer is what a program means: it does not care whether the two messages are one or two, only what the system can do in the presence of an observer. That the first projects onto the second is the whole of the claim that running the program on the store computes its meaning, and that the projection is a quotient — forgetting counts and keys — is the precise sense in which the machine knows more than the meaning does. Chapter 13’s multiplicity factor, which counted candidates by occurrence and not by term, was this quotient refused: a rate is a resource-layer quantity.

Remark 22.2 What the formal model does not cover¶

The denotation above is for monadic single-channel input with no patterns. The store the next section describes implements joins (a consume waiting on several channels at once), spatial pattern matching on the received process, persistent produces and consumes, and non-consuming reads. None of these is in the formal model; the source names joins as future work. A reader should hold the two apart: the theorem is about a fragment, and the implementation is of the whole language. That the fragment’s theorem is the right shape for the whole is likely and is not proved.

22.4 The store as built

f1r3node’s store is a Rust crate, rspace++, behind an interface generic in four types: channels \(C\), patterns \(P\), data \(A\) and continuations \(K\). For rho, \(C\) is a process (a channel is a quoted term), \(P\) is a binding pattern, \(A\) is a list of processes with a random-seed carrier, and \(K\) is a tagged continuation. The interface has a produce — put data on a channel, persistently or not — and a consume — wait on a vector of channels with a vector of patterns and a continuation, persistently or not, with a set of indices marked as non-consuming reads. Every construct of Definition 22.2 is one call: a send is a produce, a receive is a consume, and the interpreter’s reduction loop, on evaluating a send, calls produce, and on evaluating a receive, calls consume, with the optional where clause passed as a guard the matcher evaluates per candidate — which is to say that the clause of Chapter 13 already reaches the store.

Matching is the zip, with patterns.

When a produce arrives, the store looks up the joins registered on that channel, then the waiting continuations; candidates are shuffled with a random generator seeded from the deploy, the data on each joined channel is matched speculatively against its pattern, and failed attempts are rolled back. A match fires a COMM, feeding the continuation back to the interpreter with the matched data substituted. With no match, the item is stored. This is Definition 22.3 with two additions the formal model lacks — the join, which makes a redex a tuple of keys rather than one, and the pattern, which makes “matched” a structural question about the payload rather than a mere co-location — and one the formal model leaves open: the shuffle, which is the resolver, and which is seeded so that it is reproducible.

Hot store and history.

The working set is an in-memory map from channels to their data, continuations and joins. A checkpoint converts the working set’s mutations into updates of a 256-ary radix trie whose nodes are hashed with Blake2b-256, so the trie is a Merkle trie and its root is a 32-byte summary of the entire state; the trie lives in an embedded key-value store on disk, in three databases — history nodes, checkpoint roots, and cold leaf data. A checkpoint is a root hash together with the event log since the last one. That is the history monad of Chapter 12 with a particular representation: \(\Hist\) said a state carries a word of events; the trie says that the word may be summarized by a hash, that two states with the same hash are the same state, and that the summary may be recomputed by anyone holding the leaves. The representation is not incidental. It is what makes the next paragraph possible.

Replay.

A second implementation of the same interface, ReplayRSpace, takes a checkpoint and the recorded event log and forces the recorded COMMs: when the interpreter, re-running the same deploys from the same root, reaches a race, the replay store consults the log and resolves it as it was resolved the first time. Non-deterministic system processes — calls to an external model, to a network service — are replayed from their recorded outputs rather than re-executed. The replayer reaches the same root hash or it does not, and the comparison is the whole of what a validator checks. We will say more about this in the third chapter, because it is where the execution model of the shard and the three rungs of Chapter 14 meet: the executing node resolves; the replaying node observes; and a counterfactual, in Chapter 14’s sense, is a replay from a pre-state with one record changed.

Charging.

A wrapper, ChargingRSpace, meters every store operation. It pre-charges for storage when a produce or consume is stored, refunds the pre-charges when a COMM fires and charges one combined COMM cost instead, and re-charges for persistent items that remain. A field on the COMM event records whether a produce or a consume triggered it, so that play and replay — in which task scheduling may cause a different operation to trigger the same COMM — charge identically. This is the cost monad of Chapter 11 and the metered calculus of Chapter 15 running, and it is worth noticing which of their features the implementation had to get right before the book had a name for it: the order-independence of charging, which the book derives from the requirement that a validator’s bill agree with the proposer’s, and which Proposition 13.2 states as the gate deciding support while the weight distributes over it.

Merging.

A separate module analyzes event logs for conflicts: whether one event depends on another, whether two events race on a non-persistent operation, and why. This is what lets a block have more than one parent — two validators’ state changes merged when their logs do not conflict — and it is the first appearance in the implementation of the contention relation Chapter 13 defined: two events conflict when they share a datum or a receipt, and independent events commute.

22.5 What the machine knows that the calculus does not

Three facts about the store are not facts about rho, and the book’s honesty about the relationship between the two requires naming them.

The store is locked, not lock-free. The architecture document describes the store as lock-free; the code uses 4096 pre-allocated mutex stripes selected by channel hash, acquired in sorted order in two phases, with first-in-first-out waiters. Branches of a parallel composition are evaluated as concurrent tasks, and the stripes serialize their access to each channel. There is nothing wrong with this; it is how a general machine realizes a tuple space. But it means the realization has a scheduler, and the scheduler is not in the calculus. Determinism across nodes comes from three decisions, each outside rho: sends are evaluated before receives, the candidate shuffle is seeded from the deploy, and replay forces the log.

Some joins are blocked. A consume waiting twice on the same channel is refused by the implementation, because the candidate-extraction algorithm produces unmatched COMMs for overlapping patterns on one channel; and the cost of some same-channel joins involving persistent operations is acknowledged to be non-deterministic. These are documented limitations, with issue numbers. The calculus has no such restriction. The gap is small and it is real.

The bottleneck is not where one would guess. Measured, checkpoint durability is about one percent of the cost and spawning a replay runtime is under four milliseconds; the sustained throughput limit is the block replay pipeline, and the delay from block inclusion to finality is among its causes. Nothing in the repository relates the store to graphics processors or other accelerators; a design document lists them as a non-goal. The matrix note of the next chapter says why, from the other side: an RSpace-style interpreter does approximately the opposite of everything an accelerator rewards.

That last sentence is the hinge of the part. The store is mature, correct in production, and shaped for a general machine. It is a hashtable of variably shaped terms reached by pointer, matched by a recursive walk, with conflicts resolved by locks. A graphics processor wants flat indexable state, uniform work across lanes, bulk-synchronous phases, and standard sparse primitives. Both machines run the same COMM rule. The question of the next chapter is what a compiler standing between them has to do.

Remark 22.3 What this chapter claims, and at what confidence¶

Implemented and operating: every send is a produce and every receive a consume on a content-addressed store; channels are quoted processes; a rendezvous is literally the COMM rule; the whole state is summarized by one Merkle root; a validator replays the log and checks the root. Formally stated, with short proofs: the denotation is an exact monoid homomorphism, and reflection holds in the model. Formally stated, proofs sketched, obligations filed: full abstraction at both layers; the bialgebra laws; eager confluence. Not modeled: joins, patterns, persistence, peeks. The chapter has tried to keep these four registers apart on every page.
