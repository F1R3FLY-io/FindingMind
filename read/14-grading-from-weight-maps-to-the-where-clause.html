Chapter 14

Grading: From Weight Maps to the Where-Clause

14.1 What is being built, and in what order

A rewrite rule’s left-hand side is a type. Terms inhabit it; the rule fires at its inhabitants. This chapter is about what happens when one attaches a number to the firing, and it builds the attachment twice.

The first construction is the weight map. Given a continued interactive GSLT \(G\), the weight endofunctor \(\Wgt_{\Semi}\) produces a theory in which every base rule carries a finite table \[\varphi_1 \mapsto v_1, \quad \ldots, \quad \varphi_m \mapsto v_m\] from formulae refining its left-hand side to values in a semiring \(\Semi\). A term’s redexes are classified by which refinement they satisfy, and the values turn the set of enabled redexes into a distribution. Where \(\Cost\) adjoins a stack of tokens and \(\Hist\) adjoins a word of events, \(\Wgt_{\Semi}\) adjoins a table of rates — and, like both of them, it adjoins it to the state. Sections 14.2 to 14.5.1 build this and prove what it gives.

The second construction is the graded where-clause, and it is the one the rest of the book uses. It is stated in a sentence: the where clause of the for-comprehension is valued in a commutative semiring and is evaluated once per candidate match. There is no table, no key set and no partition. A programmer writes the weight where the program branches, in the same language the program is written in, and the value may depend on what was matched. Sections 14.6 to 14.9 build this.

The two are not rivals, and the order is deliberate. The weight map is the easier object to see: it is a table, the table is attached to a rule, and a simulator reads it. Having seen it, the reader can watch it dissolve. Theorem 14.4 says that a weight map is exactly a graded clause of one particular shape — a sum over a partition — so that everything Sections 14.2–14.5.1 prove about weight maps is a theorem about the linear fragment of the clause language, and the discipline those sections had to impose is the price of staying in that fragment. The reader who already knows the chapter’s destination may read the first half as an extended example. The reader who does not will find that the second half has been argued for before it is stated.

Two things distinguish both constructions from the literature on stochastic process calculi, and both are consequences of taking the weights from a logic the theory generates rather than from a vocabulary the modeler invents.

Weights depend on formulae, not names.

In Priami’s stochastic \(\pi\)-calculus [33] and in Phillips and Cardelli’s Stochastic Pi Machine (SPiM) [34], a rate is attached to a channel. That is one refinement among many: “this redex is a communication on \(n\)” is a particular formula, and there is no reason a modeler should be confined to it. Once weights are given by formulae, a rate may depend on the shape of the payload, on the namespace a channel belongs to — which in a reflective calculus is real structure, reachable through the name predicate — or on any combination of these. Channel identity is recovered as the degenerate case, and Theorem 14.2 says exactly how degenerate it is.

Weights are state, not declaration.

Rates change during execution. SPiM does not permit this, and the restriction is not incidental to its design but constitutive of it, since a static rate function is what makes the underlying object a chain over terms alone. The weight map recovers Markovianity by putting the table in the state, so the chain runs over configurations \((P,\dec)\) rather than over terms (Theorem 14.1). The where-clause recovers it more simply: a clause is re-evaluated against the current term at every candidate, so whatever a rate depends on is already in the term, and the term is the state (Proposition 14.3). Chapter 30 depends on one or the other, since plasticity is nothing other than a rate that changes.

Remark 14.1 Where the formulae come from¶

The formulae used, as keys in the first construction and as the crisp sort of the clause language in the second, are those of the logic the OSLF construction generates from the theory’s own presentation: one structural connective per term former of \(\Sigma\), one context-labeled modality \(\langle K \rangle\) per rewrite rule together with a choice of redex position, and a propositional layer stratified by the categorical strength the target specification supplies. That construction is Chapter 21, and the transition system the modalities range over is Chapter 18. Nothing in the present chapter needs the details; it needs only that the vocabulary of refinements is generated with the theory rather than designed alongside it, so that a modeler who writes down a language has, at no further cost, the language in which to price it. The one place the details do intrude is Observation 14.1, which is about what a weak target specification cannot say — and Corollary 14.2, which is about why, in the end, it does not have to.

14.2 Keys, and the partition discipline

Fix a base rule \(r : L \rsq R\). Write \(L^{\sharp}\) for the structural formula characterizing the left-hand side pattern — for the rho communication rule, “an input on some \(n\) in parallel with an output on the same \(n\)” with \(n\) existentially bound.

Definition 14.1 Refinement¶

A formula \(\varphi\) refines \(L^{\sharp}\) when \(\sat \varphi \Rightarrow L^{\sharp}\). The refinements of \(L^{\sharp}\), ordered by entailment, form the refinement lattice of the rule.

A key set is a finite subset of that lattice. Three requirements are imposed on it, and the first is the substantial one.

Definition 14.2 Partition discipline¶

A key set \(\Phi_r = \{\varphi_1, \ldots, \varphi_m\}\) is a partition of \(r\) when

  1. Exclusivity: \(\sat \neg(\varphi_i \wedge \varphi_j)\) for \(i \neq j\);

  2. Exhaustiveness: \(\sat L^{\sharp} \Rightarrow \bigvee_i \varphi_i\).

Definition 14.3 Admissible key set¶

A key set is admissible when it is a partition and in addition

  1. Decidability: each \(\varphi_i\) lies in a fragment for which model checking terminates on the terms of interest;

  2. Locality: the modal depth of each \(\varphi_i\) is bounded by some \(\alpha_r < \infty\) fixed with the rule;

  3. Structurality: each \(\varphi_i\) lies in the structural fragment, carrying no behavioral modality \(\langle K \rangle\), no greatest fixed point, and no hidden-name quantifier.

1 is forced by execution: a simulator must classify every enabled redex before it can sample, so a key whose evaluation may diverge is a key that stops the run. 2 and 3 are forced by efficient execution, and they are restrictions on keys alone — the modalities and the fixed point remain available for stating and checking properties of the resulting chain. What they may not do is determine a rate. A rate depending on what a redex can do next cannot be recomputed locally when something elsewhere in the term changes, so an implementation admitting modal keys must reclassify globally at every step. The clause language of Section 14.7 will relax exactly this, at exactly this price.

Proposition 14.1 Classification¶

Let \(\Phi_r\) be a partition of \(r\) and let \(\Red_r(P)\) be the set of redexes of \(r\) in \(P\), each a pair \((k,\sigma)\) of a position and a matching substitution. Then \[\class_r : \Red_r(P) \longrightarrow \Phi_r, \qquad \class_r(k,\sigma) = \text{the unique } \varphi_i \text{ with } L\sigma \sat \varphi_i\] is a total function, for every term \(P\).

Proof

\((k,\sigma) \in \Red_r(P)\) gives \(P \equiv k[L\sigma]\), so \(L\sigma \sat L^{\sharp}\) by construction of \(L^{\sharp}\). Exhaustiveness gives some \(i\), which is totality; exclusivity gives at most one, which is single-valuedness. Well-definedness on \(\equiv\)-classes holds because satisfaction is \(\equiv\)-invariant, the generated connectives being generated from the signature modulo the equations.

Proposition 14.1 is the whole reason for the discipline, and what it buys is visible in what fails without it. Without exhaustiveness the weighting is partial: some enabled redexes have no rate, and one must invent a convention — fire them at rate zero, at a default rate, or refuse to run — that is not part of the specification. Without exclusivity the weighting is multi-valued, and one must invent an aggregation: take the most specific key if a unique minimum exists, sum the values of all satisfied keys, take the maximum. Each convention is defensible, and each yields a different simulator from the same specification, which is precisely the situation a specification exists to prevent. Hold on to the second of those conventions — sum the values of all satisfied keys — because Section 14.8 will show that it is not a convention at all but the semantics, once one stops asking for a table.

Remark 14.2 Completing a partition is cheap¶

The discipline is less onerous than it looks. Given any pairwise-exclusive family \(\{\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_m\}\), adjoining \(\varphi_{\bot} := L^{\sharp} \wedge \neg\bigvee_i \varphi_i\) yields a partition, and a modeler wanting the unlisted redexes inert sets its value to \(0\). Exclusivity is the real constraint; exhaustiveness is a completion. In practice, keys of the form “the redex is a communication on channel \(n\)”, indexed by distinct \(n\), are exclusive for free.

14.2.1 What stating the partition costs

Definition 14.2 writes (P1) and (P2) as entailments. If they are to be stated in the generated logic itself, that logic must carry negation, conjunction and disjunction, and a target specification supplying only the finite-limits fragment will not do — finite limits supply \(\top\) and \(\wedge\) and no more (Remark 21.4).

This is a hypothesis and not a theorem about weighting as such. A key family drawn from a positive fragment may perfectly well be pairwise disjoint and jointly exhaustive; what a weak logic prevents is not the holding of (P1) and (P2) but their expression as entailments between formulae of the language the keys are written in. The alternative is to discharge them in the metatheory — by an elaborator checking them on the instances that arise, or by a proof about a schema of keys — and the weighting is then perfectly well defined. Both branches cost something, and the second cost is the interesting one.

Observation 14.1 The cost of an external partition¶

If (P1) and (P2) are discharged in the metatheory rather than in the generated logic, the specification is no longer closed: whether a key set is admissible becomes a judgment the object language cannot express. Two implementations conforming to the same written specification may then disagree about admissibility, hence — by Proposition 14.1 — about the classification, hence about the distribution sampled. That is exactly the failure the partition discipline was introduced to prevent, relocated one level up rather than removed.

So a weighted theory built on weight maps must choose between a Boolean target specification and an external admissibility judgment, and it should say which. This is a small instance of a pattern the Prestige will make central: a construction that looks self-contained turns out to have a seam, and the seam is where something the model cannot state has to be supplied from outside (Chapter 70). It is worth noticing that the seam here is visible — one can point at the entailment that has to be checked somewhere else — and that this is the good case. It is also worth noticing, in advance, that this particular seam is an artifact of asking for a table, and that Corollary 14.2 closes it.

14.3 The endofunctor

Definition 14.4 Weight map¶

Let \(G\) be a continued interactive GSLT with an admissible key set \(\Phi_r\) for each base rule \(r\). A weight map is a function \[\dec : \textstyle\coprod_r \Phi_r \longrightarrow \Semi .\] Write \(\Wmaps\) for the set of weight maps. Each \(\Phi_r\) is finite and there are finitely many rules, so \(\Wmaps \cong \Semi^N\) for a fixed \(N\).

Definition 14.5 Configuration¶

A configuration is a pair \(\mathfrak{c} = (P, \dec)\) with \(P\) a term modulo \(\equiv\) and \(\dec \in \Wmaps\). Write \(\Cfg\) for the set of configurations.

The phrase “a map instance is part of a state specification” is Definition 14.5, and everything in this half of the chapter turns on it.

Definition 14.6 Augmented rules¶

An augmented base rule is a base rule \(r : L \rsq R\) together with, for each \(\varphi_i \in \Phi_r\), an initial value \(v_i \in \Semi\) and an update function \(u_i : \Wmaps \to \Wmaps\) on the whole map. An augmented context rule is a context rule together with a geometric factor \(\gfac_c \in \Rnn\) and a fold function \(f_c\) combining the maps arriving from its sibling subterms. The initial weight map is \(\dec_0(\varphi_i) = v_i\).

Construction 14.1 The weight endofunctor¶

\(\Wgt_{\Semi}\) sends a continued interactive GSLT \(G\), equipped with admissible key sets, to the theory \(\Wgt_{\Semi}(G)\) whose configurations are the pairs of Definition 14.5 and whose rules are the augmented rules of Definition 14.6: a step is \[(P, \dec) \rewrite (P', \dec'), \qquad P' \equiv k[R\sigma], \qquad \dec' = f_{c_1}\bigl(\cdots f_{c_p}(u_i(\dec))\cdots\bigr),\] for \((k,\sigma)\) a redex of \(r\) with \(\class_r(k,\sigma) = \varphi_i\) and \(k\) composed of the context rules \(c_1, \ldots, c_p\) from the root inwards, the folds applied from the redex outwards. In the common case where every \(f_c\) projects onto its second argument — “the context does not modify the map” — this is \(\dec' = u_i(\dec)\), and we write it so below. On morphisms, \(\Wgt_{\Semi}\) acts as the identity on the underlying map of theories and by reindexing on the key sets.

The parallel with the two preceding chapters is exact, and it is the reason this chapter sits where it does. Each of the three constructions adjoins a component to the state and makes the rules act on it: a stack that drains, a word that accrues, a table that is rewritten. Each leaves the underlying interaction structure alone. And each is a genuine addition rather than a closure operation — the table after a step is not in general the table before it.

Remark 14.3 Two adjunctions and one open question¶

\(\Cost\) and \(\Hist\) are monads, resolved by free–forgetful adjunctions (Proposition 12.3, Proposition 13.1). We claim no such thing for \(\Wgt_{\Semi}\). There is an evident candidate unit: the map sending every key to \(1_{\Semi}\), which embeds \(G\) as the uniformly weighted theory in which every enabled redex is equally likely, and which is unital in the right way for the same reason the empty stack and the empty word are. Since a unit is available it might well be interesting to ask whether a multiplication exists — what it would mean to flatten a weighting of a weighting, and whether the flattening is canonical rather than chosen. We do not ask it here. Nothing below requires more than an endofunctor, and stating the construction is the point.

Remark 14.4 Why context rules carry a factor and not a propensity¶

The obvious alternative to Definition 14.6 gives every rule, base and context alike, a value, and lets the simulator select among all of them. It double-counts. A step is a base rule firing somewhere; the context rules composing the path from the root to that somewhere are the address of the firing, not additional events. Structural rules are addressing, not dynamics, and incur no primitive cost. So the context rules contribute multiplicatively, by \(\gfac(k) = \prod_q \gfac_{c_q}\) over the composition, and setting every \(\gfac_c = 1\) recovers “addressing is free” exactly, which is the default.

The reason to permit \(\gfac_c \neq 1\) is that it is the natural home of a spatial bias. \(\gfac\) weights an edge of the reduction graph by where in the term the interaction surfaces are; \(\dec(\varphi)\) weights it by what is being transferred. That is a geometry/matter split appearing here as an implementation detail, and Chapter 42 says what would have to be true for it to be more than one.

14.4 Propensity

The propensity of a rule at a refinement is where the construction is most easily got wrong, because four independent factors are involved and the literature usually presents some of them fused.

Definition 14.7 Propensity¶

Let \(\mathfrak{c} = (P, \dec)\), let \(r\) be a base rule and \(\varphi \in \Phi_r\). Put \(K(r,\varphi,P) = \{(k,\sigma) \in \Red_r(P) : \class_r(k,\sigma) = \varphi\}\). The propensity of \((r,\varphi)\) at \(\mathfrak{c}\) is \[\prop(r,\varphi,\mathfrak{c}) \;=\; \dec(\varphi) \cdot \!\!\sum_{(k,\sigma) \in K(r,\varphi,P)}\!\! \gfac(k) \cdot \chi(r,k,\sigma),\] where \(\chi \in \{0,1\}\) is the funding gate of Definition 14.8, identically \(1\) in the absence of cost accounting. The total propensity is \(\propz(\mathfrak{c}) = \sum_r \sum_{\varphi \in \Phi_r} \prop(r,\varphi,\mathfrak{c})\).

The four factors, and what each answers:

\(\dec(\varphi)\) — the rate constant.

What kind of interaction this is, priced. Dynamic: this is the component the update functions rewrite.

\(\gfac(k)\) — the geometric factor.

Where the interaction is, priced. Default \(1\).

The cardinality of the sum — multiplicity.

How many ways this interaction is available. This is Gillespie’s combinatorial count of distinct reactant tuples [3], and it is the factor most often omitted in implementations, with the consequence that a term with fifty available redexes of a kind fires them no faster than a term with one.

\(\chi\) — the funding gate.

Whether the interaction can be afforded.

Multiplicity deserves a word, because it is the one place where the associative–commutative structure of the interaction constructor does real work. Redexes are counted as selections from the bag, not identified up to \(\equiv\). Two indistinguishable messages on the same channel offer two ways for a receipt to fire, and a construction that identified them would run the system at half rate. This is not a subtlety of the implementation; it is the difference between a truncated Poisson stationary distribution and a geometric one. The clause language will make the same point in its own terms: candidates are indexed by occurrences, not by terms (Definition 14.12).

Definition 14.8 Funding gate¶

For a redex \((k,\sigma)\) of \(r\), let \(\Delta(r,k,\sigma)\) be the demand it incurs and \(\Sigma(P,k)\) the supply available at that location, in the sense of Chapter 12. Put \(\chi(r,k,\sigma) = 1\) when \(\Sigma(P,k)\) funds \(\Delta(r,k,\sigma)\) and \(0\) otherwise.

Proposition 14.2 Cost gates the support, weight distributes over it¶

Unfunded redexes have propensity zero and contribute nothing to \(\propz\), while among funded redexes the distribution is unchanged from the cost-free case up to normalization.

Proof

\(\chi\) appears as a multiplicative factor taking values in \(\{0,1\}\); a zero factor removes the term from the sum, and the remaining terms are untouched.

Three consequences are worth recording, and they are the reason this chapter belongs beside Chapter 12 rather than anywhere else in the book.

The gate is decidable, so the simulator stays exact. Were funding a real-valued penalty rather than a Boolean, the propensity would depend on an optimization and the process would no longer be exactly simulable. The decidability of the funding judgment is doing real work here.

Exhaustion is a halting condition, not a deadlock. \(\propz = 0\) when every enabled redex is unfunded. The chain has reached an absorbing state, which is the correct reading: a system that cannot pay for any of its available transitions has stopped, and the waiting time to the next event is infinite rather than undefined.

The two decorations answer different questions. Cost decides whether a step may happen; weight decides how fast and how often. Separating them is what lets a model say that a strong synapse on a starving neuron is silent — a statement neither decoration makes alone, and one Chapter 30 needs.

14.5 The simulator is a functor out of the image

\(\Wgt_{\Semi}(G)\) is still a rewrite theory: it has terms, equations and rules, and it steps. Turning it into something one can sample from is a further operation, and the two should not be run together.

Definition 14.9 The weighted coalgebra¶

Let \(\Semi^{(-)}\) denote the free-\(\Semi\)-semimodule construction, \(\Semi^{(X)}\) being the finitely supported functions \(X \to \Semi\). Evaluating Definition 14.7 pointwise gives \[\gamma : \Cfg \longrightarrow \Semi^{(\Cfg)}, \qquad \gamma(\mathfrak{c})(\mathfrak{c}') = \!\!\sum_{\substack{(r,k,\sigma) \\ \text{stepping } \mathfrak{c} \to \mathfrak{c}'}}\!\! \dec(\class_r(k,\sigma)) \cdot \gfac(k) \cdot \chi(r,k,\sigma).\]

The sum runs over derivations reaching \(\mathfrak{c}'\), not merely over the target. Assembling the weightings of a fixed theory into a category and taking the Grothendieck construction gives the fibered form of this assignment, and the simulator is a functor out of it. We record that to fix the picture and do not need it below.

Remark 14.5 Simulator and interpreter are different objects¶

It is worth saying plainly that \(\gamma\) is not what a running system does. An interpreter advances one actual state; a simulator explores how a system would behave under a range of configurations, and it is offline. That difference is what makes exhaustive construction of the transition graph, model checking, and the complex codomain of Chapter 39 viable here and not in an interpreter, and it is why the endofunctor and the coalgebra are separated above. The endofunctor adjoins the map. The coalgebra prices it.

Theorem 14.1 Markovianity on configurations¶

Sampling \(\gamma\) in the standard way — draw a waiting time exponentially with parameter \(\propz(\mathfrak{c})\), select a redex with probability proportional to its contribution, fire it — yields a time-homogeneous continuous-time Markov chain on \(\Cfg\), with generator \(Q(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{c}') = \gamma(\mathfrak{c})(\mathfrak{c}')\) off the diagonal and \(Q(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{c}) = -\sum_{\mathfrak{c}' \neq \mathfrak{c}} Q(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{c}')\). It is not in general a Markov chain on terms alone.

Proof

The propensity is a function of \(\mathfrak{c}\) and of nothing else — in particular not of the time or the history — and the successor is determined by \(\mathfrak{c}\) together with the selected redex. That is the definition of a time-homogeneous chain with the stated generator. For the negative claim it suffices to exhibit two runs reaching the same term with different maps, which Example 14.1 does.

Example 14.1 Dynamic weights are not a term-level phenomenon¶

Take rho with a single communication rule and keys \(\varphi_a, \varphi_b, \varphi_{\bot}\), where \(\varphi_x\) says the redex is a communication on \(x\). Let the updates act on \(\dec(\varphi_a)\) alone, by \(u_a : w \mapsto w+1\) and \(u_b : w \mapsto 2w\), which do not commute. A term offering exactly one \(a\)-redex and one \(b\)-redex, both of which must fire, reaches the same successor term by either order; starting from \(\dec(\varphi_a) = 1\), firing \(a\) first arrives with \((1+1)\cdot 2 = 4\) and firing \(b\) first with \(1\cdot 2 + 1 = 3\). The successor carries a single \(a\)-redex, so the two runs sit at the same term with expected waiting times \(1/4\) and \(1/3\). No rate function of the term alone reproduces both.

Theorem 14.1 is the justification for Definition 14.5, and the shape of the argument is worth being explicit about, because the design could have gone otherwise. If weight maps could change but were not part of the state, the process on terms would be non-Markovian: the rate out of \(P\) would depend on how \(P\) was reached. One would then be in the world of generalized semi-Markov processes, with no exact simulation algorithm and no chain to model check. Putting the map in the state restores exactness at the price of a larger state space, and that price is real — \(\Cfg\) is \(\terms \times \Semi^N\), a continuum unless the reachable weight maps are finite in number. Remark 14.10 will say what this example looks like once there is no table.

Remark 14.6 Self-transitions¶

Nothing forbids a rule whose firing returns a configuration \(\equiv\)-equal to the one it fired in. Such a redex contributes to \(\propz\) but to no off-diagonal entry, so the diagonal above is \(-\sum_{\mathfrak{c}' \neq \mathfrak{c}} Q\) and not \(-\propz\). A self-loop is a fictitious jump in the sense of uniformization: the two conventions induce the same distribution of the state at every time and differ only in the number of recorded events, hence in sojourn statistics. Neither is wrong; mixing them is.

14.5.1 What the restriction to names gives back

Theorem 14.2 Conservativity over summation-free SPiM¶

Restrict the construction to: the \(\pi\)-calculus fragment; keys that are channel-identity predicates and nothing else; static maps, every update the identity; and every geometric factor \(1\). Then the propensity of Definition 14.7 is exactly the activity computed by the Stochastic Pi Machine for the summation-free fragment [34], redex multiplicity included. The restriction is strict in each of the four coordinates.

We do not reproduce the proof, which is a computation matching the two definitions of activity term by term [32]; the summation correction that the general SPiM statement would need is empty here, since rho has no summation operator. The theorem is worth having for what it says about the generality: everything the construction adds beyond SPiM is visible as the removal of one of four restrictions, and each removal is independently motivated. Formula-keys remove the first, plasticity the second, spatial bias the third.

That completes the first construction. Everything above is correct, and everything above is about to be shown to be a special case.

14.6 Where the choice is

A rho program does not determine its own execution. Write \[\begin{equation} x!(Q_1) \mid \textsf{for}(y \leftarrow x)\,P \mid x!(Q_2) \end{equation}\] and the operational semantics permits two reductions. It does not say which one happens, and it does not say with what tendency. This is not an oversight. It is the defining feature of a mobile process calculus, and it is what makes the calculus a specification of interaction rather than of a machine.

An implementation must close the gap. RSpace — the store that realizes rho in f1r3node, which Part Part II examines in its own right — closes it by presenting the parallel fragment as a table from channels to bags of data and bags of continuations, and letting the physical race between cores decide which produce is zipped with which consume. That is a resolution mechanism, and it is a choice, in the precise sense Chapter 67 will make exact: the family of admissible pairings, indexed by contended channels, has a section only when something supplies one.

The observation on which this half of the chapter rests is that the resolution mechanism is a parameter, and that other values of the parameter are available. Gillespie’s algorithm is a resolution mechanism: it supplies rates for the branches of a race and an exponential clock for turning them into outcomes. The Born rule is a resolution mechanism: it supplies amplitudes and a squared modulus. The weight map of the preceding sections is a way of setting the parameter from outside the term — a table beside the program. The question this half answers is what syntax a programmer needs in order to set it from inside.

14.6.1 Why contexts cannot see the parameter

Before building the syntax it is worth knowing what kind of thing the parameter is, and the cleanest way to say it is to say what cannot detect it.

Principle 14.1 Contexts create apertures; they do not read them¶

A rho context can change which races exist — by adding contention, by consuming a contender, by installing a new receive. It cannot observe how any race is resolved. Consequently no property of the form “for all rho contexts \(C[-]\), …” can be sensitive to the resolution mechanism.

The reason is almost a tautology once stated: a rho context is itself a rho term, whose own races are resolved by the same unspecified mechanism. Composing silence with silence yields silence. The consequence is that the correctness criterion the rest of this part of the book leans on — full abstraction against the class of contexts, as in Chapter 22 — is not the right instrument here. It is not that there are too few contexts. It is that the discriminating power one wants is not of the kind contexts have.

Two things survive. The first is a different correctness frame: the object of study is the map \[\textsf{Resolvers} \times \textsf{Terms} \longrightarrow \textsf{Distributions over outcomes},\] in which the RSpace race, the Gillespie clock and the Born rule are three points of the first coordinate, and the interesting statements are separations between resolvers at a fixed term rather than between terms at a fixed resolver. The second is a one-sided obligation that every instantiation must meet.

Definition 14.10 Support adequacy¶

Let \(\Reach(P)\) be the set of normal forms operationally reachable from \(P\) under the unweighted semantics, and let \(\sem{P}\) be a weighted denotation of \(P\) with values in \(\Semi\). The denotation is support-adequate when \[\supp\,\sem{P} \;\subseteq\; \Reach(P).\]

Support adequacy says the resolver invents nothing the operational semantics forbids. The converse inclusion is not demanded, and for a reason that will matter in Chapter 39: a normal form that some derivation reaches, whose contributions cancel, is exactly what a resolver with additive inverses can produce. So the natural two-sided condition is not merely unproven; in the one instance where it fails, its failure is the physics.

Remark 14.7 Non-compositionality is not a defect¶

The store denotation of rho is a monoid homomorphism for parallel composition, because it denotes the store. A denotation that resolves races cannot be, because composing \(P\) with \(Q\) creates races that neither had alone. The cut is the interaction term, and a dependence across the cut that neither side carries is the semantic image of a race across the cut. This is the same observation as Chapter 65’s: an aperture is a property of the cut and of neither agent flanking it. It is recorded here because the reader trained on denotational semantics will otherwise read the failure of compositionality below as a mistake.

14.7 The graded where-clause

14.7.1 Resolution algebras

Definition 14.11 Resolution algebra¶

A resolution algebra is a commutative semiring \(\Semi = (V, \sumv, \tensorv, 0, 1)\): \(\sumv\) associative and commutative with unit \(0\), \(\tensorv\) associative and commutative with unit \(1\), \(\tensorv\) distributing over \(\sumv\), and \(0\) annihilating.

The two operations are forced by what the logical connectives must mean once they take values. Conjunction is \(\tensorv\): two conditions holding together compose multiplicatively, which is also what makes a joined receive \(p_1 \leftarrow c_1 \ \&\ p_2 \leftarrow c_2\) combine its clauses correctly. Disjunction is \(\sumv\): two ways for a match to be enabled add. This is the same semiring that Section 14.3 took as a codomain; what has changed is that it is now the value space of a logic, and the logic’s connectives are its operations.

Principle 14.2 Interference is a logical phenomenon¶

Since \(\sem{\varphi \vee \psi} = \sem{\varphi} \sumv \sem{\psi}\), in a resolution algebra with additive inverses a disjunction may evaluate to \(0\) though neither disjunct does. Destructive interference is a false disjunction with true disjuncts. This is the whole of the additional structure, stated in the logic rather than imposed on it from outside.

Negation is the casualty and it is worth saying so plainly. On \([0,1]\) the map \(v \mapsto 1 - v\) serves; on \(\CC\) there is no canonical complement and De Morgan fails against \((+,\times)\). The design therefore keeps two sorts of formula, with the crisp sort embedded in the graded one.

14.7.2 Two sorts, one slot

Let the crisp sort be the condition language the book already has: ground formulae supplied by a language definition, the spatial connectives generated from the term formers, the context-labeled modality \(\langle K \rangle\), and full Boolean structure. \[\begin{align*} \beta &::= \textsf{true} \mid \textsf{false} \mid g \mid \neg\beta \mid \beta \wedge \beta \mid \beta \vee \beta \mid \langle K \rangle\beta \mid \textsf{out}(n)\beta \mid \textsf{in}(n)\beta \mid \beta \mid \beta \mid @\beta\end{align*}\]

and let the graded sort be

\[\begin{align*} \psi &::= \crisp{\beta} \mid v \mid \psi \tensorv \psi \mid \psi \sumv \psi \mid \langle K \rangle_{\Semi}\,\psi \qquad (v \in V). \end{align*}\] Here \(\crisp{\beta}\) is the indicator embedding, sending a satisfied \(\beta\) to \(1\) and an unsatisfied one to \(0\), and \(v\) ranges over scalar constants of the algebra. The graded sort has no negation and no fixed-point operator; the crisp sort keeps both.

Remark 14.8 One slot, not two¶

It is tempting to give the for-comprehension two clauses, a Boolean gate and a graded weight. It is better to give it one graded clause and recover the gate as its support: a communication is enabled exactly when its clause evaluates to something other than \(0\). This keeps the language unforked — the Boolean where-clauses rholang already has are the \(\Bool\)-valued fragment under \(\Bool \hookrightarrow \Semi\) — and it makes support adequacy (Definition 14.10) hold by construction rather than by proof, since the classical interpreter sees nothing of the value except whether it vanishes.

14.7.3 Syntax

The for-comprehension already carries a where clause, whose condition may mention the variables bound by the preceding receipts. The change is only to the sort of that condition.

for( p11 <- c11 & ... & pm1 <- cm1 ;
     ... ;
     p1n <- c1n & ... & pmn <- cmn
     where psi ) P

with psi a graded formula over the algebra \(\Semi\) declared for the shard or for the channel’s language definition. Within psi the bound names \(p_{ij}\) are in scope, so the value may depend on what was matched. That one sentence is the whole of the difference between a clause and a key: a key classifies a redex by a formula fixed in advance; a clause computes a value from the match in front of it.

14.7.4 Per-match evaluation

The decisive point concerns when the clause is evaluated.

Definition 14.12 Candidates¶

Let \(r\) be a receipt site in \(P\) — a for-comprehension together with the channels its receipts name. A candidate at \(r\) is an assignment \(\sigma\) of a distinct datum occurrence to each pattern of each group, such that every pattern matches its assigned datum. Write \(\Cand(P,r)\) for the set of candidates and \(\Out(r,\sigma) \in \terms/{\equiv}\) for the residual term the firing of \(r\) under \(\sigma\) produces.

Candidates are indexed by occurrences, not by terms. This is the multiplicity factor of Section 14.4 appearing in its native form: two structurally congruent messages on a channel are two candidates, not one, because parallel composition is a multiset and structural congruence does not delete duplicates. An implementation that keys data by content alone cannot represent \(\Cand\) faithfully.

Definition 14.13 Graded clause valuation¶

A graded clause \(\psi\) at a receipt site \(r\) denotes a function \[\sem{\psi}_r : \Cand(P,r) \longrightarrow V ,\] defined by structural recursion, with \(\crisp{\beta}\) evaluated against the term \(P\) under the substitution \(\sigma\) carries, scalars constant, \(\tensorv\) and \(\sumv\) pointwise, and \(\langle K \rangle_{\Semi}\) as in Section 14.7.5.

So a clause is a vector indexed by candidates, and — once composed with \(\Out\) — a matrix over candidates and outcomes. That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth pausing on how little it is. The weight map needed a key set, a partition discipline, an admissibility judgment, a classification function and an update function. The clause needs a semiring and a scope rule.

14.7.5 The graded modality is a transfer operator

Let \(\Semi\langle \terms/{\equiv}\rangle\) be the free \(\Semi\)-semimodule on terms modulo structural congruence. Define the one-step operator of a term by summing its candidates: \[\begin{equation} T(P) \;=\; \sum_{r}\ \sum_{\sigma \in \Cand(P,r)} \sem{\psi_r}_r(\sigma)\;\cdot\;\Out(r,\sigma) , \end{equation}\] the outer sum over receipt sites and the coefficient arithmetic in \(\Semi\). Then the graded modality is \[\sem{\langle K \rangle_{\Semi}\,\psi}(P) \;=\; \sum_{r,\sigma} \sem{\psi_r}_r(\sigma) \tensorv \sem{\psi}(\Out(r,\sigma)),\] which is \(T\) acting on the vector of values of \(\psi\). The graded modality is the transfer matrix of the reduction relation, and the principle that the meaning of a term is the sum of all its possible futures is exactly the statement that \(\sem{-}\) is a fixed point of \(\langle K\rangle_{\Semi}\). The denotational principle and the graded logic are one object seen twice.

This is also where the chapter’s own restriction 3 is repaid. A key could not carry a behavioral modality because a rate depending on what a redex does next cannot be recomputed locally. A clause may carry \(\langle K\rangle_{\Semi}\), and the price is exactly the one named there: such a clause is not local, and an implementation must either bound its modal depth or re-evaluate it globally. The restriction has not gone away; it has become a choice the programmer makes in the clause, with a cost the ledger can see.

14.7.6 Denotation and conservativity

Definition 14.14 Graded denotation¶

Fix a resolution algebra \(\Semi\) and a budget \(\beta \in \mathbb{N}\). Let \(M := \Semi\langle \terms/{\equiv}\rangle\) with basis vectors written \(\lvert P \rangle\). The \(\beta\)-budgeted denotation is \[\sem{P}_\beta \;:=\; T_{\!*}^{\,\beta}\,\lvert P \rangle \;=\;\!\!\sum_{h \,:\, P \Rightarrow^{\leq\beta} Q}\!\! w(h)\,\lvert Q\rangle ,\] where \(T_*\) extends \(T\) linearly, \(h\) ranges over causal histories of length at most \(\beta\) ending at a normal form or exhausting the budget, and \(w(h)\) is the \(\tensorv\)-product of the clause values along \(h\). At a normal form \(Q\) the operator acts as the identity, \(T\lvert Q\rangle = \lvert Q\rangle\), so that normal forms are absorbing.

Remark 14.9 Why a budget, and only one¶

The definition is corecursive and does not bottom out on non-terminating terms. For terminating \(P\) the recursion is well-founded on the reduction tree and \(\beta\) may be taken to be its depth. In general one needs either a convergence hypothesis — a spectral radius condition on \(T\), since \(\CC\) has no order and Knaster–Tarski is unavailable — or a budget. We take the budget, and note that it is the budget this book already charges: the risk ledger’s single \(\omega\) for reflection (Chapter 64), and the token budget that keeps the mortal scientist’s inquiry terminating (Chapter 26). These should be one budget doing several jobs rather than several budgets.

One denotation admits three readings, differing only in what is done at the end. Support: \(\supp(\sem{P}_\beta)\), which is what the classical interpreter sees and, by Remark 14.8, all it sees. Stochastic: for \(\Semi = \Rnn\), the coefficients normalized, which is the jump-chain marginal of the Gillespie reading. Born: for \(\Semi = \CC\), the squared moduli normalized, which is Chapter 39.

Theorem 14.3 Conservativity¶

Let \(\Semi = \Bool\) and let every clause be the embedding \(\crisp{\beta}\) of a crisp condition. Then \(\supp(\sem{P}_\beta)\) is exactly the set of normal forms reachable from \(P\) in at most \(\beta\) steps under the operational semantics with Boolean where clauses.

Proof

In \(\Bool\), \(\tensorv = \wedge\) and \(\sumv = \vee\), so \(w(h) = 1\) iff every step of \(h\) has a satisfied clause, and the coefficient of \(\lvert Q\rangle\) is the disjunction over histories reaching \(Q\). Candidates coincide with the admissible zips of the store presentation, and the absorbing convention does not add normal forms. Hence the coefficient is \(1\) iff some admissible derivation reaches \(Q\).

Corollary 14.1 Support adequacy¶

For any \(\Semi\), \(\supp(\sem{P}_\beta) \subseteq \Reach_\beta(P)\), since a nonzero coefficient requires at least one history with all clause values nonzero, and such a history is admissible under the Boolean image of the clauses. Equality fails exactly when some reachable coefficient is a sum of nonzero terms summing to \(0\).

This is the payoff of Remark 14.8, and it should be read as a safety theorem. Adding grading cannot make a program do something it could not do; it can only make it fail to do something it could. Everything the ecology part builds on top of the clause inherits this: a learner whose hypotheses are clauses can be wrong, but it cannot be wrong in a way the unweighted calculus forbids.

14.8 Weight maps are the linear graded clauses

The first half of this chapter can now be placed.

Theorem 14.4 Weight maps are a normal form¶

Every weight map \((\varphi_i, v_i)_{i\leq k}\) on a rule is denoted by the graded clause \[\psi \;=\; \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} \; v_i \tensorv \crisp{\varphi_i},\] and conversely a graded clause of this shape denotes a weight map if and only if the \(\varphi_i\) partition the refinement of the rule’s left-hand side. Graded clauses not of this shape — those with nested \(\tensorv\) over overlapping conditions, or with a graded modality — are strictly more expressive.

Proof

Forwards: on a candidate satisfying \(\varphi_j\) and no other, all but the \(j\)th summand vanish and the value is \(v_j\), and exhaustiveness of the partition ensures some summand survives. Backwards: if two \(\varphi\)’s overlap on a candidate, the clause returns \(v_i \sumv v_j\), which is a well-defined value but not a table lookup; the partition condition is exactly the condition making the sum a selection. Strictness: with overlapping \(\varphi_i\) the clause is a mixture no weight map assigns, and \(\langle K\rangle_{\Semi}\) inspects successors, which no key can.

The theorem is the hinge of the chapter and it is worth saying what it does to each piece of the first half. The weight map is the linear fragment of the clause language: the clauses that are sums of scalars times indicators over a partition. The classification function \(\class_r\) is the statement that on such a clause exactly one summand is nonzero. The propensity formula of Definition 14.7 is Equation (14.2) with the clause in normal form and \(\Out\) grouped by outcome. And the convention Section 14.2 declined — “sum the values of all satisfied keys” — is not a convention: it is what \(\sumv\) means, and the partition discipline is what was needed to stop it from ever being invoked.

Corollary 14.2 The partition discipline is unnecessary¶

Single-valuedness of a graded clause is automatic, a formula having one value. The requirements that key sets partition the left-hand-side refinement (Definition 14.2), that admissibility be checked (Definition 14.3), and that a default class complete them (Remark 14.2), all exist to secure single-valuedness for a lookup and have no analogue here.

This dissolves the obstruction of Section 14.2.1. The Boolean demand — that the target logic specification supply negation so that a partition can be stated — arose because a partition needs complements. With no partition to define, the demand does not arise. Negation is still absent from the graded sort, but it is absent as a design choice about the value algebra rather than as an unmet requirement, and the seam Observation 14.1 pointed at is no longer there to point at. The reader should notice what kind of repair this is. Nothing was proved that the weight map could not prove; a presupposition was removed, and the problem it generated left with it. The Prestige will have occasion to recall the shape.

Proposition 14.3 Plasticity is free¶

A dynamic weight map, whose table is revised after each rewrite by an update function \(u : \Wmaps \times \textsf{Match} \times \textsf{Trace} \to \Wmaps\), is denoted by a static graded clause whenever \(u\)’s dependence on the trace is expressible as a crisp formula over the current term. No update function is needed, because a clause is re-evaluated against the current term at every candidate.

Proof

The clause mentions the term through its crisp subformulae; its value at a candidate in the term \(P'\) reached after some history is by definition computed against \(P'\). A table revised to depend on features of \(P'\) therefore agrees with the clause \(\bigoplus_i v_i \tensorv \crisp{\varphi_i}\) in which the \(\varphi_i\) mention those features directly. Dependence on trace history beyond what is visible in \(P'\) is not expressible, which is the hypothesis.

Remark 14.10 The state is the term¶

Proposition 14.3 has a hypothesis, and Example 14.1 is a case where it fails: the two orders of firing leave the same term and different tables, and no formula over the term can tell the orders apart. The weight map’s answer was to enlarge the state to \((P,\dec)\) and prove Markovianity there. The clause’s answer is the one rho always gives: put it in the term. A rate that must remember something the term does not show is a rate that depends on a datum nobody wrote down; write it down — a message on a channel the clause can read — and the clause reads it. The non-commuting updates of the example become two rewrites that each consume and re-emit a counter, and the chain is a chain on terms again, because the term now carries what the table carried. In a calculus where the state is the term, “the map is in the state” and “the map is in the term” are the same sentence, and the configuration of Definition 14.5 was a way of saying it before the syntax existed to say it directly. Chapter 30 will make this move for synaptic weights, and the finiteness it needs is supplied by the same device that supplies it there: a quantized value on a bounded range.

Remark 14.11 What was retired, and what was kept¶

The update function of Definition 14.6 was the one component of the weight map with no compilable form: an arbitrary closure over time and a trace summary, for which a declarative sub-language would have had to be designed. The graded clause is that sub-language, and it was needed for other reasons. The geometric factor \(\gfac\) and the funding gate \(\chi\) are kept exactly as they were: the first because it weights the address of a firing rather than the match, which a clause at the site cannot see; the second because, as Proposition 14.2 says, cost is not a weighting and should not be dressed as one. The endofunctor \(\Wgt_{\Semi}\) is kept as the name for the linear fragment, which is what the physics part of the book instantiates.

14.9 Contention, and what is normalized

One more notion is needed before the clause can be handed to the chapters that use it, and it is the notion the weight map never had to name because the partition hid it. Equation (14.2) sums over receipt sites as well as over candidates, and that outer sum conflates two different things: candidates at different sites may be alternatives, or they may be concurrent, and only the first should be weighed against each other.

Definition 14.15 Conflict, components, matchings¶

Two candidates conflict when they share a datum or share a receipt. A contention component at \(P\) is a connected component of the conflict relation. A step of a component is one of its maximal matchings: a maximal set of pairwise non-conflicting candidates.

The terminology is that of event structures, where conflict names events that cannot both occur [279]. A contention component is the unit of choice: within it, the alternatives are its maximal matchings, which is the RSpace zip — the bag of data and the bag of patterns, admissibly paired — and across components nothing is chosen because everything fires. Two independent races are two components; the race equation (14.1) is one component with two matchings; a receipt with \(m\) patterns and \(m\) data is one component with \(m!\) matchings.

Proposition 14.4 Components factor¶

Let \(C_1,\dots,C_k\) be the contention components at \(P\). The maximal matchings of \(P\) are exactly the elements of \(\prod_i \mathrm{Match}(C_i)\): a step chooses one matching per component, independently, and all of them fire.

Proof

Candidates in distinct components neither share a datum nor share a receipt, so a set of candidates is pairwise non-conflicting iff its restriction to each component is; maximality is likewise componentwise.

Why this matters is that normalization — turning values into odds — happens over a component and nowhere else. In the Gillespie reading of Section 14.5.1 this was invisible, because exponential clocks race globally and the total propensity does the normalizing for free. In the discrete stochastic reading, where a clause’s values are relative odds rather than rates, the odds of a matching are its weight divided by the sum over the matchings of its component; and in the complex reading, the norm that must be preserved is taken over a component after summing amplitudes over matchings with a common outcome. Both of those sentences will be said again, with their consequences, in Chapters 39 and 36. Here it is enough to record two honest costs.

Remark 14.12 Two costs¶

Normalization is nonlocal: a clause can be normalized only in the light of what else is contending, so a normalized value is a property of a configuration and not of a clause. And the sum over maximal matchings of a component is permanent-shaped, hence \(\#P\)-hard in general — which is a fact about the physics it models and bites an implementation, so what a compiler checks must be a syntactic sufficient condition such as a bound on component size. Neither cost arises in the linear fragment, because a partition is a component structure imposed in advance. That is a fair statement of what the weight map bought and what it cost: tractability, at the price of forbidding every race the modeler did not foresee.

14.10 Where this is used

The construction is generic over the theory: it applies to anything one can write down as a language presentation, so one obtains a stochastic machine for the ambient calculus, for a user-defined domain language, or for cost-accounted rho, by writing the theory rather than by writing a simulator.

Five places in this book take it up, and the division of labor between the two halves of the chapter is worth stating. Chapter 15, next, builds the first thing a clause can do that a table cannot — an operator that intervenes on a race, under which Pearl’s calculus of causation embeds exactly. Chapter 30 runs the clause at the smallest scale where the whole apparatus is visible at once, and finds a spiking neural network with plasticity in it — synaptic efficacy as a graded clause, and the same rewrite that performs inference performing the learning. Chapter 26 takes the clause as the form of a hypothesis: a mortal scientist’s conjecture about its world is a graded clause, and what it learns is a value in the fiber over that clause. Chapter 36 asks which semiring, in the linear fragment, and finds that the choice is what separates nondeterminism from cost from probability from amplitude. And Chapter 39 asks what the complex instance means, and finds that the clause takes the one route the weight map could not — amplitudes attached to candidates rather than to classes — and meets, in Definition 14.15, exactly the structure that decides whether the result is a quantum state.
